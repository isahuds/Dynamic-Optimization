Planning without known cross sections

You do not need σ in advance. You need to count.

Both cross sections are usually unknown before the first campaign on a new part, which appears to make the model unusable. It does not, for two separate reasons.

The observable cross section is not needed for the decision

\(\sigma_{\text{circ}}\) scales how many events you collect, but it cancels out of the optimum entirely: \(\phi_{\text{opt}}=u^{*}/(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}W)\) does not contain it. A wrong guess changes the predicted duration of your campaign, not the flux you should choose. Put in an order-of-magnitude estimate, choose your operating point, and ignore the absolute event counts until you have measured it.

The SEFI cross section does not need to be known, only counted

The quantity that actually sets the operating point is \(x\), the number of interrupts per work cycle. That is directly observable during the test without knowing \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) at all: count interrupts, count work cycles attempted, divide.

  1. Start at a deliberately low flux, wherever you would have started conservatively anyway.
  2. Run long enough to log at least 20 to 30 interrupts.
  3. Compute \(x_{\text{obs}}\) and scale flux by \(x_{\text{target}}/x_{\text{obs}}\), in steps.
  4. Recount after each step. If \(x\) stops tracking flux proportionally, you have left the regime where \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) is flux-independent, and you should stop there.

As a by-product you get \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}=x_{\text{obs}}/(\phi W)\) for free, which is a publishable measurement in its own right.

A safe target when you know nothing

The yield curve is flat near its peak, so a fixed target for \(x\) does nearly as well as the true optimum across a very wide range of recovery costs. Each cell is the fraction of best achievable throughput obtained by targeting that fixed \(x\).

Target \(x\approx0.3\). It stays within roughly 15% of optimal over a 300-fold range in recovery cost, and requires no prior knowledge of either cross section. If recovery is cheap, automatic and in-beam, drift toward 0.5. If it needs a person and a paused beam, drift toward 0.15.

Erring low is much safer than erring high. The curve falls gently below the optimum and steeply above it, and the model's assumptions are the ones that break first at high flux.

If you must plan entirely on paper

When even a pilot is not possible, bound the problem rather than guessing a point value.

  • Bracket \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) from published data on a similar part. Functional-interrupt cross sections for commercial microcontrollers at saturation commonly fall within about \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm^{2}}\). Plan at the pessimistic end, since the penalty for over-driving is much steeper than for under-driving.
  • Use a Weibull curve if one exists. \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) enters only as \(\phi_{\text{opt}}\propto1/\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\), so a published fit gives a flux-versus-LET plan directly: high flux at low LET, backing off as the cross section saturates.
  • Design the work cycle short. \(W\) is the one term entirely under your control before the beam is on. A short cycle keeps \(x\) low whatever \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) turns out to be, and can be lengthened later once you have measured it.
  • Budget the first hour as measurement. Treat the pilot as part of the plan rather than as overhead. One hour spent establishing \(x\) can reposition the remaining campaign by a factor of several in yield.
