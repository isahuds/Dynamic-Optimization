What this page does

A work cycle survives when it finishes without a functional interrupt. Its chance of doing so depends on the flux, the length of the cycle, and one cross section for losing a cycle, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}\). A target share of surviving cycles \(\theta\) therefore fixes the product of flux and work-cycle time. Read it one way and it is the flux to run for the work cycle you have; read it the other way and it is the longest work cycle a flux you have already chosen allows.

The survival form is the one used for checkpointed computation (Young 1974; Daly 2006). Choosing flux from it is the method of Zimmaro et al. (RADECS 2022), which this site's companion paper tests against a direct count of surviving cycles on heavy ions and extends. It sits alongside established guidance that bounds flux to keep a measurement free of artifacts (Buchner et al. 2002; Berg et al. 2009; Quinn 2014). This page charges nothing for recovery time; the recovery page does. Full citations are on the evidence page.

The share of attempted work cycles you want to survive. The choice is yours. The companion paper illustrates with 0.90. Zimmaro et al. (RADECS 2022) arrive at \(\theta\) through the flux choice itself, maximizing the chance of seeing a rarer failure first. Coronetti et al. (2021), for system-level testing, recommend a flux that lets at least ten passes through the system's operating modes complete between two hard losses of functionality, those needing a power cycle to clear.

The rule

$$\theta = e^{-\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}\,\phi\,W} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \phi\,W = \frac{-\ln\theta}{\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}}$$

Every pair of flux and work-cycle time with the same product delivers the same share of surviving cycles, so a flux ten times higher is paid for by a work cycle ten times shorter. \(\phi W\) is the fluence one cycle receives.

Why long work cycles pay more for flux

Because the exponent scales with \(\phi W\), pushing flux costs a long cycle far more than a short one. In the companion campaign, one \(\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}\) described six builds with work cycles from 20 to 814 ms. At \(2\times10^{5}\) cm⁻² s⁻¹, four times the flux they ran at, the same fit puts the longest cycle at 8% surviving and the shortest still at 94%. At the matched flux and \(\theta=0.90\), the longest usable cycle is 135 ms. Three of the six builds sit under it, FRAM_B50 exceeds it by a factor of 1.6, and the two at \(B=200\) by a factor of about 6.

Where a curve crosses your target \(\theta\), its \(W\) is that flux's longest usable work cycle.

Which cross section to use

Only a cross section counted from lost cycles over in-cycle fluence belongs in the rule. A functional-interrupt cross section from a reset counter, or one from the host's recovery record, counts over the full beam-on fluence, which includes the time outside work cycles spent handling interrupts: recovering from them, waiting to detect them, and re-running the cycles they cut short. Either will predict more surviving cycles than a test delivers, by an amount that grows with how much time the device spends outside its work cycle. In the companion campaign the host's record gave \(\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}=8.3\times10^{-6}\) cm², a rate of 0.42 s⁻¹ against the fitted 0.78 s⁻¹, and predicted a higher surviving share than every build measured.
