Dynamic SEE Test Design Optimizer

Choose beam flux and firmware duty cycle to maximise verified data per hour of beam time.

What this tool answers

Beam time is expensive and finite. In a dynamic single-event effect (SEE) test the device under test must stay running, but a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) destroys whatever work was in progress and costs recovery time. Push the flux too high and the device spends the campaign recovering instead of producing verifiable results. Push it too low and you pay for beam you barely use.

This tool takes six measurable parameters and returns how much verified data your current setup produces per hour, what flux would maximise that, and how many hours of beam time you would save.

Everything runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded.

When recovery happens under irradiation, a further interrupt during recovery restarts it, so the effective recovery time is \(R_{\text{eff}} = (e^{\lambda R}-1)/\lambda\) rather than \(R\). This grows very sharply with flux and is the term that stops the model recommending arbitrarily hot beams. Unticking it is the optimistic assumption and will overstate the flux you should run.

What counts as good performance

Two numbers decide whether a test design is well matched. Both are reported above.

1. Interrupts per work cycle, x

This is the single most useful diagnostic. It says how many interrupts you expect during one attempt at producing a result. It is dimensionless, and it has a natural target near 0.2 to 0.6 for most recovery costs.

Value of xMeaningWhat to do
below 0.05Under-driven. Almost every cycle completes, but you are buying more beam time than you need.Raise flux, or lengthen the work cycle.
0.05 to 1.0Well matched. You lose some cycles, but the throughput gain outweighs the loss.Fine tune toward u* shown above.
above 1.0Over-driven. Most cycles are destroyed before they finish and the campaign turns into a recovery loop.Reduce flux, shorten the cycle, or make recovery cheaper.

2. Throughput relative to achievable, ηrel

The percentage of the best possible data rate you are currently getting, holding everything fixed except flux. Above 90% means there is little left on the table and you should spend effort elsewhere. Below 50% means flux is genuinely mis-set and correcting it is the cheapest available improvement.

Improving the parts that flux cannot fix

Optimal flux only rescues the third factor in the model. The other two are design choices:

  • Raise f. Every additional percent of the duty cycle spent in the circuit of interest converts directly into an equal percent more data, with no downside from interrupts. Throttling the computation clock relative to the communication clock is one way to do this; it makes the compute phase occupy more of the cycle.
  • Lower R. Automatic in-beam resynchronisation instead of a manual reset with the beam paused is usually worth far more than any flux adjustment, because R both reduces throughput and forces the optimal flux downward.

The model

Per work cycle, interrupts arrive as a Poisson process at rate \(\lambda=\phi\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\). A cycle survives with probability \(e^{-x}\), and costs \(W\) if it survives or \(W+R\) if it does not. Dividing the fluence banked per cycle by the average cycle length gives the effective fluence rate delivered to the circuit of interest:

$$\dot{\Phi}_{\text{eff}}=\phi\,f\,\eta(x,\rho),\qquad \eta(x,\rho)=\frac{e^{-x}}{1+\rho\left(1-e^{-x}\right)}$$

with the two dimensionless groups

$$x=\phi\,\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\,W \quad\text{(interrupts per cycle)},\qquad \rho=\frac{R}{W}\quad\text{(recovery cost ratio)}$$

Raising flux multiplies the first factor but shrinks the third, so a maximum exists. Setting the derivative with respect to \(\phi\) to zero gives

$$(1-u^{*})(1+\rho)=\rho\,e^{-u^{*}},\qquad \phi_{\text{opt}}=\frac{u^{*}(\rho)}{\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\,W}$$

The limits behave as expected. If recovery were free, \(\rho\to0\) and \(u^{*}\to1\): accept about one interrupt per cycle. If recovery is very expensive, \(\rho\to\infty\) and \(u^{*}\to0\): keep interrupts rare. Observation rate follows by multiplying the effective fluence rate by the cross section of whatever you are measuring: \(\;\text{events s}^{-1}=\dot{\Phi}_{\text{eff}}\,\sigma_{\text{circ}}\).

Validation

The targeting-efficiency term was tested against a heavy-ion campaign on the MSP430FR6989 at Michigan State University's Facility for Rare Isotope Beams. Two firmware builds provide a controlled comparison: identical storage placement, identical batch size, identical communication clock, differing only in computation clock (16 MHz against 1 MHz), run at the same LET and at fluxes within 3% of each other.

The model predicts a 7.25× efficiency ratio from duty cycle alone. The observed ratio was 6.28×, and the implied circuit cross sections agree to within 13%, which is the behaviour expected if the difference is observation efficiency rather than underlying susceptibility.

What this evidence does and does not establish

An independent audit of this comparison found three limitations that materially weaken it, and they are stated here rather than buried:

  • The test has little statistical power. With 28 and 7 observed errors, the exact 95% confidence interval on the ratio is 2.7 to 17.0. The predicted 7.25 sits inside it, but so does 16, which is the clock-frequency ratio. The data therefore support the weaker statement that computation-phase errors grow with time spent computing, and cannot by themselves distinguish scaling with duty cycle from scaling with clock rate.
  • The agreement depends on the event-selection rule. Restricting to errors with no interrupt or persistence context gives 13% disagreement, but that cut removes 30% of one configuration's errors and 56% of the other's. Including persistent errors gives 58% disagreement, and including all computation-phase errors gives 46%. The headline figure is the most favourable of several defensible choices.
  • The confound is not separable in this pair. Duty cycle and clock rate move together by construction, so the two explanations make no differing prediction here. One weak argument favours the duty-cycle account: single-event transient capture generally becomes more likely at higher clock frequency, which predicts fewer errors at 1 MHz, the opposite of what was seen. Separating them properly needs a configuration where duty cycle varies at fixed clock.

The model's structure is sound and its algebra has been independently verified. Its empirical support at present is a single under-powered comparison, so the tool should be used to rank candidate test designs rather than to certify a predicted yield.
