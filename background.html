Derivation

The device repeats a work cycle of duration \(W\) containing computation and telemetry. A fraction \(f\) of that time exercises the circuit of interest, so the fluence arriving at that circuit during one cycle is \(\phi f W\).

Functional interrupts arrive as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda=\phi\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\), so a cycle survives to completion with probability

$$P_{\text{survive}}=e^{-\lambda W}=e^{-x},\qquad x\equiv\phi\,\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\,W$$

A cycle that survives costs \(W\). A cycle that does not costs \(W\) plus recovery, so the expected duration of one cycle is \(W+(1-e^{-x})R_{\text{eff}}\). Renewal-reward gives the rate as expected reward per cycle divided by expected cycle length:

$$\dot{\Phi}_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\phi f W\,e^{-x}}{W+(1-e^{-x})R_{\text{eff}}} =\phi\,f\,\underbrace{\frac{e^{-x}}{1+\rho(1-e^{-x})}}_{\eta},\qquad \rho\equiv\frac{R_{\text{eff}}}{W}$$

So \(\eta\) is simply the probability a cycle survives, divided by the average cycle length in units of \(W\). Three sanity checks: \(x=0\) gives \(\eta=1\) and you keep everything; \(\rho=0\) gives \(\eta=e^{-x}\) and you only lose the failed cycles; large \(x\) drives \(\eta\to0\) as the device thrashes.

The optimum

Since \(x\propto\phi\), raising flux multiplies the leading factor linearly but shrinks \(\eta\) exponentially. Differentiating \(u\,\eta(u)\) and setting it to zero gives

$$(1-u^{*})(1+\rho)=\rho\,e^{-u^{*}},\qquad \phi_{\text{opt}}=\frac{u^{*}(\rho)}{\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}W}$$

Read \(u^{*}\) as the optimal number of interrupts to accept per cycle. The root is unique in \((0,1)\) for every \(\rho>0\), and it is a maximum rather than an inflection. The limits behave correctly: free recovery gives \(u^{*}\to1\), ruinous recovery gives \(u^{*}\to0\).

Recovery is not free from the beam

The most consequential refinement is that \(R\) is not constant. If the beam stays on during recovery, as it does for in-beam automatic resynchronisation, an interrupt during recovery restarts it. For a restart-on-failure process of nominal length \(R\) under Poisson hazard \(\lambda\), expected completion time is

$$R_{\text{eff}}=\frac{e^{\lambda R}-1}{\lambda}$$

which diverges sharply as \(\lambda R\) approaches unity. This term is what stops the model from recommending arbitrarily hot beams, and it changes conclusions rather than refining them.

For the campaign on the validation page, treating recovery as protected suggests raising flux 5.3× for a 1.36× gain. Accounting for exposure, nominal recovery of 2.5 s becomes 5.6 s at the current flux and roughly 670 s at the "recommended" one, and the correct advice inverts to lowering flux by about 1.5×. The same mechanism explains the sustained interrupt clusters seen in that campaign, which a constant-\(R\) model simply cannot produce.

If the beam is genuinely paused, or the device shielded, for the whole recovery sequence, then \(R_{\text{eff}}=R\) and the closed form applies. That is the only case where the optimistic setting is correct.

Relationship to checkpointing

The structure is the accelerated-testing analogue of the optimal checkpoint-interval problem in fault-tolerant computing. There, a long-running job checkpoints periodically; too-frequent checkpoints waste time on overhead, too-rare ones lose more work per failure. Young's first-order result and Daly's higher-order refinement solve that trade-off, and the same renewal-reward reasoning underlies both.

One difference is worth stating. In computing, the failure rate is a property of the environment and only the checkpoint interval can be chosen. A radiation test engineer also controls the failure rate itself, through flux. That extra degree of freedom is why the optimisation here runs over \(\phi\), while the classical result runs over the interval. The batch size page recovers the classical direction: with flux fixed, choosing \(B\) is choosing how much work to risk between commits.

Assumptions, and where each one breaks

AssumptionBreaks whenConsequence
Interrupts are Poisson with constant rate The device enters sustained clustering, or susceptibility depends on internal state Optimal flux is overestimated. The \(R_{\text{eff}}\) term captures part of this but not all.
\(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\) is independent of flux High flux changes the failure mode, for example by preventing recovery from completing The model extrapolates past the point where its own inputs stop being constant. Step flux gradually and recount \(x\).
An interrupt destroys the whole cycle Partial results remain usable, or work is committed incrementally Model is pessimistic; true yield is higher, and larger batches are safer than predicted.
A failed cycle consumes its full duration \(W\) The system aborts promptly on detection Slightly pessimistic. Correction is about \(0.5(1-e^{-x})W\), around one percent near the optimum.
\(f\) is a property of the configuration Duty cycle shifts with failure rate, for example through retries \(f\) measured under heavy interrupt load is contaminated. Measure it at low flux.
Recovery cost \(R\) is a single number Automatic and manual recovery differ by orders of magnitude Use the occurrence-weighted mean, and check sensitivity; the optimum moves with \(R\).

The model is best used to rank candidate designs and to identify the direction and approximate scale of an improvement. It is not a substitute for a qualification test plan, and it should not be used to certify a predicted yield.
