The flux-primary formula: why one number isn't enough

A work cycle of length \(W\) survives an interruption-free run with probability \(e^{-\lambda W}\). Pick a target clean-cycle fraction \(\theta\) and solve for the interruption rate that hits it:

$$\lambda_{\text{target}}=\frac{-\ln(\theta)}{W}.$$

That gives one pull toward low flux: fewer interruptions, more of your work cycles survive clean. The other pull comes from wanting to reach a target fluence \(\Phi\) in minimum wall-clock time, \(T=\Phi/\phi\), which favors high flux with no ceiling of its own. It is tempting to look for a flux that balances both at once, but they do not meet in the middle. Using the verified-output rate \(\dot N_V\) below, the number of clean cycles accumulated by the time a fixed fluence target is reached keeps improving as flux falls toward zero. An unboundedly long, unboundedly low-flux campaign racks up unboundedly many clean cycles. There is no interior flux that is simultaneously best for both objectives.

That is why \(\theta\) has to come from the experimenter, not from the physics. Fixing a target clean-cycle fraction turns an open-ended tradeoff into a single well-posed answer: the highest flux consistent with your own quality bar, and the wall time that flux implies. Loosening \(\theta\) trades coverage for speed. Tightening it does the reverse. Neither direction is a mistake. It is a choice this page can only surface, not make for you.

Pileup, in plain terms

At high enough flux, a new ion can strike while the device is still recovering from the last one. That second strike does not register as a separate interruption, because there was no running work cycle left for it to interrupt. The device was already down. So the measured interruption rate \(\lambda\) does not climb forever with flux. It rises, peaks, and can fall again, even though the true rate of physical strikes keeps climbing.

This campaign measured that curve directly, with 35 runs spanning a 3-decade flux range, fitted to \(\lambda(\phi)=k\phi e^{-k\phi\tau}\) below. Most test campaigns will not have the beam time for a sweep like that, and do not need it. The practical version: pilot at a candidate flux, measure \(\lambda\) directly, and watch what happens as you raise flux across a couple of pilots. If \(\lambda\) keeps climbing, you are still on the safe side. If it flattens or drops, you have crossed into pileup. Back off rather than push further, since the apparent time savings from more flux is not real past that point.

This is also where the two formulas above meet. If your target \(\lambda_{\text{target}}\) is higher than the interruption rate ever gets even at the pileup peak, your clean-cycle target was never the binding constraint. Pileup fidelity is the binding constraint instead, and the honest recommendation is to cap flux at the peak (or comfortably below it), not to claim an unreachable target is satisfied. The pre-campaign tool handles this distinction automatically and tells you which constraint is actually binding for your numbers.

Flux and pileup

The trigger rate depends on beam flux through a paralyzable pileup mechanism:

$$\lambda(\phi)=k\phi\,e^{-k\phi\tau}$$

Fitted to the within-firmware subset with the widest flux range (35 runs, \(1.1\times10^{3}\) to \(1.2\times10^{5}\) cm\(^{-2}\)s\(^{-1}\)):

ParameterValueInterpretation
\(k\)\(1.072\times10^{-4}\) cm²per-ion trigger cross section in the small-flux limit
\(\tau\)0.4275 sdead time per trigger (paralysis window)
\(\phi^{*}=1/(k\tau)\)\(21{,}826\pm1{,}448\) cm\(^{-2}\)s\(^{-1}\) (6.6%, delta method)peak trigger rate (pileup onset)

Exposure time here is a per-run measure built from logged compute-call windows, more precise than a single facility-reported elapsed value; the coarser measure gives a statistically indistinguishable fit (\(k=1.069\times10^{-4}\), \(\tau=0.4406\) s, peak \(21{,}234\pm1{,}438\)), with a slightly worse \(R^2\) (0.366 vs. 0.386 below), so the more precise measure is used throughout.

The fit quality is \(R^2=0.386\), which is the best among the models tried (flat-saturation \(R^2=0.11\), power law \(R^2=0.004\)) but not a strong absolute fit, and fragile in its parameter estimates specifically: two individual runs account for most of the gap to \(R^2=0.44\) when either is excluded, shifting \(k\) and \(\tau\) by 15% and 9%. The model choice itself is not fragile the same way. Refitting with both runs excluded still favors this paralyzable form by a wider margin, not a narrower one. The fitted peak falls inside the untested gap between the campaign's low- and high-flux clusters.

The key insight for design: a rate measured at one flux does not transfer to another. A short pilot at the flux you intend to run is essential.

Why this tool never recommends a flux above \(\phi^*\). The clean-cycle fraction \(\theta=e^{-\lambda W}\) is computed from whichever \(\lambda\) you give it, and \(\lambda\) itself is suppressed by pileup once flux passes \(\phi^*\): a device that is failing to keep up looks quieter, not louder, because closely spaced triggers merge into fewer counted recoveries. Computing \(\theta\) from that suppressed rate does not merely lose precision near the peak; it gets systematically more optimistic exactly where it should become less trustworthy, since the formula has no way to know the rate it was given is already an undercount. This is not hypothetical: at one campaign configuration's own tested flux, 2.3\(\times\) its fitted peak, the model predicts the observed rate to within 7.1% (the fit is trustworthy at this exact point, not a wild extrapolation), and the reported clean-cycle fraction from raw \(\lambda\) was 0.965; the same fit's intrinsic, pre-suppression rate implies the true figure is 0.676, a 28.9-percentage-point gap and a 9.3\(\times\) understatement of the interrupted-cycle fraction. Across the full 35-run sweep this gap is near zero below the fitted peak and grows past it, reaching 0.60 (0.986 reported vs. 0.387 intrinsic) at the sweep's highest-flux tail. A leave-one-out check on the two most influential fit points (above) moves these numbers to roughly 0.25–0.33 (published 0.29), 8.1–10.4\(\times\) (published 9.3\(\times\)), and 0.54–0.65 (published 0.60) respectively — stable in direction and rough scale, not pinned down to better than about \(\pm15\%\). This is why the flux recommendation only ever searches the rising branch, up to \(\phi^*\), returning the peak itself as a flux ceiling rather than a flux past it: any \(\theta\) computed from an observed rate above \(\phi^*\) cannot be trusted at face value. If you independently compute a clean-cycle fraction from a rate measured above this device's own fitted peak, elsewhere or with a different device, treat it the same way.

Why "just exclude recovery time" doesn't fix this

A natural next idea: if recovery time is what's being suppressed into the observed rate, why not define exposure time to exclude it, and fit \(k\)/\(\tau\) against an "up-time-only" denominator instead? This was checked directly. The pileup shape survives under that alternative denominator (paralyzable still wins, \(R^2=0.307\) vs. \(0.386\)), and the peak flux itself is nearly unchanged (19,742 vs. 21,826, about 7%, each within the other's 95% confidence interval) — but \(k\) and \(\tau\) individually shift by roughly 53% and 30% and remain strongly anti-correlated under both conventions (\(\rho\approx-0.92\)). Peak flux, not \(k\) or \(\tau\) alone, is the portable quantity across exposure-time conventions.

More fundamentally, the exclusion is circular for this experimental design: how much time to exclude for a given interruption depends on the same interruption rate the fit is trying to estimate, so building it into the fit's own denominator feeds the model its own answer as an input. Excluding recovery time from the accounting while the beam and device both stay engaged the whole time does not change what the device was actually exposed to — only physically removing the device from the beam during recovery (for example, an automated actuator that withdraws the device on fault detection and returns it once recovery is confirmed) breaks the circularity, because it removes real exposure rather than relabeling its accounting. Doing this does not eliminate the blind spot above; it shrinks the risk to the actuator's own detection latency, the delay between a fault occurring and the actuator acting on it, which this campaign never measured directly and which would need to be weighed against the added mechanical and accounting complexity of repeated pause/resume cycles.

Rate hierarchy

Three rates appear in this project and are not interchangeable:

SymbolNameWhat it countsStatus
\(\lambda\)Operational trigger rateWork-interrupting triggers per beam-burst second; every trigger costs one recovery whether or not it corresponds to an independent physical initiationCalibrated
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{loss}}\)Verified-service loss hazardFirst entries into a grouped work-loss episode from online-verified productive service, over detector-live verified-productive timeUncalibrated (no compatible at-risk clock)
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{SEFI}}\)Physical SEFI rateDevice-side initiation evidence with independence adjudicationWithheld

The design model uses \(\lambda\), not \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SEFI}}\). This is deliberate. The trigger rate is what actually interrupts work and determines throughput, regardless of whether each trigger corresponds to a physically independent event.
