What this page adds

The first page asks how many attempted cycles come back clean. This page asks how much of the time you book turns into countable exposure. Each lost cycle also costs dead time: beam-on time from the interrupt until the device resumes work, during which the host cannot observe it. Dead time has two parts, and a pilot can measure both: the detection latency, the host's wait to detect the interrupt, and the recovery that follows it. The companion paper names these two measurements, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}\) and your target \(\theta\), as the three measured inputs and one choice that set a dynamic test's flux and work cycle.

Setting \(\tau=0\) returns the first page's answer exactly: the published relation (Zimmaro et al., RADECS 2022), in which dead time is free. See the references.

Duty factor

$$D=\frac{\theta W}{W+(1-\theta)\tau} \qquad \theta=\frac{D\,(W+\tau)}{W+D\,\tau}$$

\(D\) is the share of the time spent running cycles or in dead time that yields countable exposure, meaning fluence delivered during cycles that survive to report. By default only the recovery part of dead time is charged to \(D\), so it is an upper bound on the share of beam-on time that counts. The host's wait to detect an interrupt is dead time too, and in the companion campaign, adding the measured detection latency to \(\tau\) charged it as well, bringing \(D\) within 0.02 to 0.05 of the directly measured share at \(B\le50\). Re-running the cycles that interrupts cut short is not dead time — the host observes the device throughout a re-run — but a re-run does not begin from a verified state, so it is not counted as a work cycle either, and none of that time is charged.

The charge a test pays is \((1-\theta)\tau/W\). A short cycle loses a correspondingly smaller share of its cycles, so to first order \(W\) drops out and the charge is left to \(\tau\). A build already keeping most of its cycles gains more from recovering reliably than from shortening the cycle it runs.

A caution on hidden strikes

A second strike that lands before the host has detected and recovered from the first resets the device again, but opens no new recovery episode. The device's reset counter records it; the host does not. This is the dead-time counting loss known from radiation detectors (Knoll 2010), and Zimmaro et al. (TNS 2022) measured it as masking at high flux on a system recovered by an external watchdog.

In the companion campaign, 11 to 13% of the interrupts in the four FRAM builds at the reference condition were hidden this way, so their true interrupt rate likely lies 12 to 16% above the host-detected rate. Most of that loss arose during the detection latency, and only 2 to 3 points arose during recovery. A shutter closed on detection keeps recovery out of the exposed window (Wilcox et al. 2017; Texas Instruments 2019), so what remains to plan for is the detection latency, the input above.

Hidden strikes bias a host-detected interrupt rate low, and with it a projected mission interrupt rate. They do not bias \(\theta\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}\) or the corruption-event rate: a hidden strike lands in dead time, after its cycle is already lost. See the evidence.

What the campaign measured

Six builds at one flux and LET. The two that keep the most cycles, FRAM_B1 and SRAM_B1, rank only third and fourth on duty factor: dead time reorders them. Charged for the recovery part of dead time alone, 34 to 72% of the time spent running cycles and recovering yielded no countable exposure, depending on the build.

Most recoveries took the one 0.165 s settle, and most of each build's recovery time went to the recoveries whose first attempt failed: 15 and 22% of recoveries for FRAM_B1 and SRAM_B1, against 6 to 11% for the rest. A failed first attempt costs further attempts or, when resynchronization fails outright, the host's resynchronization timeout. That timeout is the host's own choice, distinct from the detection latency above.

Where beam-on time went

ActivityShare of beam-on time in a call

Re-running is not dead time, since the host observes the device throughout, but a re-run does not begin from a verified state, so it is not counted as a new work cycle. Measured directly over beam-on time, the countable share was 12 to 42%, below every build's \(D\). Adding the measured detection latency, 0.7 to 1.1 s per lost cycle, to \(\tau\) brings \(D\) within 0.02 to 0.05 of that share at \(B\le50\), for example 0.39 against 0.37 for FRAM_B1. At \(B=200\), re-running cut-short cycles took about 40% of beam-on time, none of it charged to \(D\).
