How much of this is distinguishable?

The central values above invite a configuration-by-configuration reading that the counting statistics do not support. Of the fifteen pairwise comparisons of \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\), nine have overlapping 95% intervals. The factor of 2.5 spread across the set is real at the extremes, since SRAM_1 and SRAM_ctrl_200 are separated, but most individual pairs are not resolved and should not be treated as different.

The same caution applies to the computation-phase cross sections, where the counts are much smaller:

Two consequences follow. First, the 13% central agreement between FRAM_1 and FRAM_1_throttled cited below is far more precise than the data warrant: those two intervals overlap across most of their range. That agreement is consistent with the model, but it is weak evidence for it. Second, the SRAM_1 result is the one configuration contrast in the set that survives its own intervals, lying wholly above FRAM_1_throttled and marginally above FRAM_1.

This is why the batch series carries the argument. It spans two orders of magnitude in \(x\), which is far wider than the counting uncertainty on any individual point, so its trend does not depend on resolving configurations that the statistics cannot separate.

1. Batch size, the widest test

The all-FRAM batch series spans two orders of magnitude in \(x\) at essentially fixed flux, which makes it the most demanding test available in this campaign.

The work cycle grows about seventyfold from \(B=1\) to \(B=200\), so \(x\) crosses unity and survival collapses. Both 200-batch builds sit near two expected interrupts per attempted batch, and between them produced two verified computation errors across the whole campaign against 28 for the batch-1 throttled build. The original analysis attributed this to long telemetry intervals; the model reaches the same conclusion quantitatively, as a direct consequence of \(W\).

Fitted to the \(B=1\) and \(B=50\) points, the batch model puts the optimum near \(B\approx40\) for this campaign. At that setting \(B=1\) returns about a quarter of the achievable rate, \(B=50\) is within one percent of best, and \(B=200\) falls away sharply. The measured \(W\) at \(B=200\) was also about 70% longer than linear extrapolation predicts, itself consistent with failed batches being retried.

2. Targeting efficiency, the controlled pair

FRAM_1 and FRAM_1_throttled use identical storage placement, batch size and communication clock. They differ only in computation clock, 16 MHz against 1 MHz. The model predicts that computation-phase error rate scales as \(f\,\sigma_{\text{circ}}\), so fluence-per-error multiplied by \(f\) should match across the pair.

Predicted ratio 7.25× from duty cycle alone, observed 6.28×, implied cross sections agreeing within 13%. That is the expected behaviour if the difference is observation efficiency rather than underlying susceptibility.

What this evidence does not establish

  • Little statistical power. With 28 and 7 observed errors the exact 95% interval on the ratio is 2.7 to 17.0. The predicted 7.25 sits inside it, but so does 16, the clock ratio. The data support the weaker claim that computation-phase errors grow with time spent computing, and cannot separate duty-cycle scaling from clock-rate scaling.
  • Sensitive to event selection. Restricting to errors with no interrupt or persistence context gives 13% disagreement, but that cut removes 30% of one build's errors and 56% of the other's. Including persistent errors gives 58%; including all computation-phase errors gives 46%. The headline is the most favourable of several defensible choices.
  • Confound not separable here. Duty cycle and clock rate move together by construction. One weak argument favours the duty-cycle account: single-event transient capture generally becomes more likely at higher clock frequency, predicting fewer errors at 1 MHz, the opposite of what was seen. Separating them needs \(f\) varying at fixed clock.

3. SRAM_1 separates susceptibility from observation efficiency

SRAM_1 has almost the same \(f\) as FRAM_1, 0.048 against 0.045, and a slightly lower \(\sigma_{\text{SEFI}}\). If observation efficiency were the whole story they should collect errors at similar rates. They do not: SRAM_1's implied \(\sigma_{\text{circ}}\) is 3.68×10−5 cm2, about five times the FRAM value. Because SRAM_1 relocates input, output and control storage into volatile SRAM rather than merely changing timing, that residual is the expected signature of genuinely higher storage susceptibility.

Separating \(f\) from \(\sigma_{\text{circ}}\) is what makes the distinction visible; a raw error-rate comparison would have confounded them. With seven observed errors from a single run this is suggestive rather than established.

4. A limitation exposed by the 50-batch configuration

FRAM_50 sits at \(x=0.167\), which by the model makes it the best-placed configuration in the campaign. Its measured behaviour does not match that description, and the reason is a specific limitation of the model rather than a defect in the data.

Flux is not the explanation. The three FRAM_50 runs delivered 45.2, 45.3 and 48.5 k ions cm−2 s−1, matching the facility record to better than 1% and sitting slightly below the campaign mean.

What the outputs show is that 358 of 573 compared batches contained verified mismatches, with 17,607 of 17,900 compared rows in error, against only 80 logged interruptions in the same runs. That is roughly 4.5 corrupted batches per interruption. Grouped into persistent episodes: 126 episodes, of which 125 begin after a recovery, and 39 are runs of entirely zero-valued output extending up to 27 consecutive batches. Every persistent all-zero cluster anywhere in the campaign occurs in this one configuration.

The model assumes an interruption destroys the cycle it lands in and no more. FRAM_50 violates that: a reset re-establishes the communication protocol without reinitialising the FRAM-resident output and control arrays, so corrupted state survives recovery and is transmitted again. The effect peaks at this batch size because a 50-result batch is long enough for the device to complete and report further batches while still carrying corrupted state. The 200-batch builds mostly fail to complete a batch at all, and the batch-1 builds clear within one cycle.

Consequence for test design. \(x\) ranks exposure to interruption, not the yield of usable data, when recovery does not restore internal state. A configuration can sit at a favourable \(x\) and still deliver little verified output. Where non-volatile storage holds computation state, measure how long wrong output persists after a recovery, and prefer a configuration that clears within one cycle over one that does not at equal \(x\).

5. Independent audit of the model itself

A separate skeptical audit was run against the derivation and the validation, instructed to find errors rather than confirm them.

Confirmed correct

  • The renewal-reward derivation. Expected cycle length \(W+(1-e^{-x})R\) with reward \(e^{-x}\phi f W\) gives exactly the published expression.
  • The optimum. The derivative's numerator is algebraically identical to \((1-u)(1+\rho)=\rho e^{-u}\); the root is unique in \((0,1)\) for all \(\rho>0\) and is a global maximum, verified against grid search over \(\rho\) from \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{8}\).
  • Non-circularity of the validation. Fluence-per-error uses all-phase fluence, so multiplying by \(f\) is legitimate rather than double-counting.

Found wrong, and corrected

The original model treated recovery cost as constant, which produced a recommendation to raise flux 5.3×. Recovery under an active beam is interruptible, giving \(R_{\text{eff}}=(e^{\lambda R}-1)/\lambda\). Nominal 2.5 s becomes 5.6 s at the campaign flux and roughly 670 s at the originally recommended one, at which point recovery cannot statistically complete. The correct advice inverts to lowering flux by about 1.5×. The tool now defaults to the exposed-recovery assumption, and the mechanism also explains the campaign's own sustained interrupt clusters.

Open items

  • Fluence normalisations are mixed between sources; measured interrupts per cycle (0.0348) and modelled \(x\) (0.0416) differ by 20%. Measuring \(x\) directly is preferable.
  • \(W\) in the reference implementation is pooled across all LETs, 72 ms against 84.6 ms for this LET alone.
  • The recovery cost of 2.49 s is not directly traceable to the logs; logged resynchronisation per interrupt is 0.37 s. The conclusion shifts with this value.

The model's structure and algebra are independently verified. Its empirical support is one wide but coarse batch sweep plus one under-powered controlled pair. Use the tool to rank candidate designs, not to certify a predicted yield.
