Flux, fluence, and work cycle

Flux (\(\phi\))

How intense the beam is: how many ions are hitting each square centimeter of the device every second. It is a rate, and on this site it is held fixed for the whole duration of one run.

Technical: ions·cm\(^{-2}\)·s\(^{-1}\). Flux is the free input to the pre-campaign tool's flux recommendation and the independent variable in the pileup model \(\lambda(\phi)=k\phi\,e^{-k\phi\tau}\).

Fluence (\(\Phi\))

The total exposure delivered over an entire run: how many ions per square centimeter landed on the device from start to finish. Where flux is a rate (per second), fluence is a running total with no time dimension. It is what you accumulate, not how fast you accumulate it.

Technical: ions·cm\(^{-2}\), typically the target quantity for a qualification campaign (e.g. \(10^{7}\) cm\(^{-2}\)). Fluence and flux relate through wall-clock time: \(\Phi=\phi T\). See prospective statistics for how fluence enters as the exposure denominator in a cross-section calculation.

Work-cycle time (\(W\))

How long one checkpoint-to-checkpoint interval takes on the bench, when nothing goes wrong. You measure this directly with a stopwatch on your own test article. It is not something the model derives for you.

Technical: \(W\) is the free-running cycle duration in seconds. In the site's general flux-primary formula, \(W\) is treated as a single measured, application-fixed number and is not assumed to decompose into a batch size. Only the optional batch-size refinement assumes the finer-grained \(W(B)=W_0+Bt_s\), and that decomposition is specific to test architectures with a tunable checkpoint interval. See Model & caveats for the full renewal-reward derivation.

Interruption rate and pileup

Interruption / trigger rate (\(\lambda\))

How often, per second, something interrupts the device's work and forces a recovery. This is an operational count of interruptions actually observed, not a claim about the underlying physical event rate.

Technical: \(\lambda\) is the rate of work-interrupting triggers per beam-burst second. It is a deliberate proxy for the true single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) rate, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SEFI}}\), not identical to it. Every trigger costs one recovery whether or not it corresponds to an independent physical initiation. See the rate hierarchy in Model & caveats for why the design model deliberately uses \(\lambda\) rather than \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SEFI}}\).

Clean-cycle fraction (\(\theta\))

The probability that one work cycle finishes start-to-end without getting interrupted. This is the adjustable quality-target slider on the pre-campaign tool. Pick how clean you want your cycles to be, and the tool backs out the flux and rate that achieve it. The default of 80% is a reasonable starting point built into the tool, not a claimed community standard.

Technical: \(\theta=e^{-\lambda W}\), so the required interruption rate for a target \(\theta\) and measured \(W\) is \(\lambda=-\ln(\theta)/W\).

SEFI (Single-Event Functional Interrupt)

A radiation-induced upset that disrupts how the device is operating, not just a bit flipping somewhere in memory, but the device's function actually breaking, such that it needs some form of recovery (automatic reset, resynchronization, or manual intervention) before it can resume useful work.

SEU (Single-Event Upset)

A single bit or memory element flipping state due to a radiation strike. An SEU does not necessarily disrupt device operation the way a SEFI does. A flipped bit in an unused memory location may never be observed functionally, while a SEFI by definition interrupts work in progress.

Pileup

At high enough flux, a second ion can strike the device while it is still recovering from the first one. That second strike does not register as its own separate interruption, because the device was already down. The practical consequence: the observed trigger rate \(\lambda(\phi)\) does not keep climbing forever as flux increases. It rises, peaks, and then falls, even though the true underlying susceptibility has not changed.

Technical: pileup is modeled as a paralyzable process, \(\lambda(\phi)=k\phi\,e^{-k\phi\tau}\), fitted on this campaign's data with \(R^2=0.386\) (outperforming flat-saturation and power-law alternatives). See flux and pileup and the flux relationship evidence.

Dead time (\(\tau\))

The paralysis window right after a trigger, during which the device is busy recovering and any further strikes go uncounted as separate interruptions. This is the mechanism behind pileup.

Technical: fitted campaign value \(\tau\approx0.4275\) s (0.4275 s in the model constants). Larger \(\tau\) means pileup sets in at lower flux.

Pileup onset / peak flux (\(\phi^{*}\))

The flux at which the observed trigger rate is at its highest. Below this flux, a higher beam intensity gives you a proportionally higher, trustworthy trigger rate. Above it, the device is spending so much of its time recovering that the operational rate stops faithfully tracking the device's true susceptibility. Pushing the flux higher there gets you fewer, not more, resolvable events.

Technical: \(\phi^{*}=1/(k\tau)\), fitted to \(\approx2.2\times10^{4}\) cm\(^{-2}\)s\(^{-1}\) (21,826 cm\(^{-2}\)s\(^{-1}\) in the model constants) on this campaign. The fitted peak falls inside an untested gap between the campaign's low- and high-flux clusters, so treat it as an estimate to be confirmed with a pilot run, not a certified boundary.

Susceptibility (\(k\))

How trigger-prone the device is, per ion, when flux is low enough that pileup is not yet a factor. This is meant to be a property of the device itself, roughly independent of how hard you're running the beam.

Technical: \(k\) is the per-ion trigger cross section in the small-flux (linear) limit of the pileup model, fitted to \(k\approx1.072\times10^{-4}\) cm\(^2\) (1.072\(\times10^{-4}\) in the model constants) on the within-firmware subset with the widest flux range. See Model & caveats for the fit details.

Batch size (optional)

Batch size (\(B\))

How many results the device produces between one checkpoint and the next, for test setups where that checkpoint interval is something you can actually configure. This is a secondary, optional lever. If your test architecture has a fixed, non-configurable work cycle, batch size is not a knob you have, and you don't need it. Just measure \(W\) directly as described under work-cycle time above and use the flux-primary formula on the pre-campaign tool.

Technical: for architectures that do support a tunable checkpoint interval, work cycle decomposes as \(W(B)=W_0+Bt_s\), where \(W_0\) is fixed overhead and \(t_s\) is the marginal per-result cost. The calibrated campaign values are \(W_0\approx25.1\) ms and \(t_s\approx7.1\) ms.

\(B^{*}\)

The batch size that squeezes the most verified throughput out of a flux you've already chosen, for architectures where batch size is tunable. It is presented on this site as an upper bound, not a single recommended number. Bias your actual choice toward the lower half of a factor-of-two window around it.

Technical: \(B^{*}=(\sqrt{2W_0/\lambda}-W_0)/t_s\), derived from the renewal-reward model under the assumption that batch-validity probability \(p_V\) is independent of batch size. The campaign's heterogeneity finding gives reason to doubt that assumption, and the correction is sign-determinate. If \(p_V\) actually declines with batch size, the true risk-aware optimum sits at or below \(B^{*}\), never above it. See the \(p_V(B)\) caveat for the full argument.

Recovery cost / mean recovery time (\(\bar R\))

The average time it costs, in seconds, to recover from one interruption. A slower recovery makes every run take longer in absolute terms, but it does not change which batch size or flux is best. It scales the whole throughput curve down without moving its peak.

Technical: \(\bar R\) enters only the denominator of the verified-output-rate expression \(\dot N_V(B)=\dfrac{Bp_V}{\mathrm{expm1}[\lambda W(B)]\cdot[1/\lambda+\bar R]}\). It does not appear in the stationary condition that determines \(B^{*}\). See Recovery for the campaign's measured recovery-time distributions.

Time, cross section, and LET

Wall-clock time (\(T\))

The actual real-world time a run will take to reach a target fluence at a chosen flux, which is what shows up on the beam-time invoice.

Technical: \(T=\Phi/\phi\), the direct consequence of flux being a rate and fluence being its time integral.

Cross section (\(\sigma\))

A per-ion measure of how likely a strike is to cause a countable event, normalized so it can be compared across different flux and fluence conditions.

Technical: an operational cross section (events per unit fluence, from whatever count and exposure were actually used) is not automatically the same as a physical cross section (tied to a specific, independence-adjudicated device mechanism). Unless a page states otherwise, the rates and cross sections on this site are operational. See the flux relationship for the campaign's fitted operational scaling, \(\sigma\propto\phi^{-0.96}\).

LET (Linear Energy Transfer)

How much energy an incident ion deposits per unit path length as it passes through the device, usually expressed in MeV·cm\(^2\)/mg. It is the standard measure of how "heavy," in effect, a given ion strike is, and is the usual x-axis for reporting SEE sensitivity across different ion species.

Statistics

Garwood interval / zero-event upper limit

The standard way to put an honest confidence interval on a count of rare events, including the awkward case where you observed zero events and still need to say something quantitative about how rare the underlying rate could plausibly be.

Technical: for a positive event count, this site uses the two-sided exact Garwood interval at 95% confidence, built from the chi-square quantile function. For zero counts, no two-sided interval is defined, so the site reports the one-sided 95% upper limit \(-\ln(0.05)/\Phi=2.995732273553991/\Phi\) instead. Both conventions are applied only after a count passes the seven eligibility gates described in prospective statistics. A count that fails those gates is withheld rather than converted into a number.
